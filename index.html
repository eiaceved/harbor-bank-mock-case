01

What technology risk means at EY

Technology risk is the chance that the systems and data a business runs on get breached, break down, or break the rules. The job is finding those risks and making sure the right controls are in place.

Put simply, EY's technology risk teams help clients answer one question: can we trust our systems and our data, especially while the business is changing?

IT audit and assurance

Supports the financial statement audit

  • Test IT general controls behind financial systems.
  • SOX section 404 IT testing for public companies.
  • SOC 1 and SOC 2 reports for service providers.

Technology risk consulting

Helps clients manage risk as they change

  • Cyber and identity risk assessments.
  • Third-party, or vendor, risk management.
  • Risk in cloud, ERP, and AI rollouts.
  • Resilience: outages, backups, disaster recovery.
  • Data privacy and regulatory readiness.

What a first-year actually does

  • Walkthroughs. Sit with an IT owner and trace how a control works, step by step.
  • Testing. Pull a sample, say 25 change tickets, and check each one for approval.
  • Documenting. Write up what you saw and what you tested in workpapers.
  • Findings. Explain the gap, the risk, and the fix in plain business language.

The risk lifecycle, the loop behind every engagement

  1. IdentifyWhat could go wrong?
  2. AssessHow likely, and how bad?
  3. RespondAvoid, mitigate, transfer, or accept.
  4. MonitorAre controls still working?

↺ Then repeat as the business changes

Ten terms to drop naturally

IT general controls (ITGCs)

Baseline controls over IT, in four domains: access to programs and data, program changes, program development, and computer operations.

Risk = likelihood × impact

How likely is it, and how bad would it be? Plot both on a heat map to prioritize.

Inherent vs. residual risk

Inherent is the risk before controls. Residual is what is left after controls.

Preventive vs. detective

Preventive controls stop a problem, like multi-factor login. Detective controls catch it after, like a weekly log review.

Four risk responses

Avoid it, mitigate it, transfer it (for example, insurance), or accept it.

Least privilege

People and vendors get only the access they need, only for as long as they need it.

NIST CSF 2.0

A widely used cybersecurity framework with six functions: Govern, Identify, Protect, Detect, Respond, Recover.

SOC 1 vs. SOC 2

SOC 1 covers controls that matter to a customer's financial reporting. SOC 2 covers security, availability, processing integrity, confidentiality, and privacy.

Three Lines model

First line: management owns the risk. Second line: risk and compliance oversee it. Third line: internal audit gives independent assurance.

COSO and COBIT

COSO is the standard internal control framework. COBIT is its IT governance counterpart. Refer to both as control frameworks.

03

Clarifying questions

Your move

Ask two or three clarifying questions. Pick ones whose answers would change your approach.

Strong questions, with the interviewer's answers

  • What does success look like: a yes-or-no, or a prioritized plan?
    A ranked list of top risks and a 90-day plan the board can approve.
  • When does the cloud migration go live?
    The cutover is planned for six months from now.
  • Has the bank had any recent incidents?
    Yes. Last quarter, a fintech vendor's account was used to get into a test environment. No customer data was lost, but it rattled leadership.
  • Is there a budget for new controls?
    About 3 million dollars a year.
  • Are regulators watching anything in particular?
    Bank examiners will look closely at third-party and cyber risk.

Skip questions that don't change your approach, like how many employees the bank has. Two or three sharp questions beat six generic ones.

04

Build the framework

Interviewer

Great. How would you structure your approach to this problem?

Your move

Take 60 seconds to sketch three or four buckets, then walk the interviewer through them out loud.

Strong framework

Can Harbor grow digitally without unacceptable technology risk, and what should it fix first?
1Growth plan and risk appetite
  • What's changing: new app, fintech partner, cloud
  • How much risk the board will tolerate
  • What regulators expect
2Identify the risks
  • Identity and access
  • Third-party (vendor) risk
  • Change and resilience
  • Cloud configuration
  • Data and privacy
3Assess and prioritize
  • Incident trends
  • Control testing (ITGCs)
  • Likelihood × impact
  • Dollar exposure
4Respond and roadmap
  • Fixes, cost vs. risk reduced
  • 30/60/90-day plan
  • Owners and risk indicators

Say it like this

"I'd break this into four parts. First, I'd understand the growth plan and the bank's risk appetite: what's changing, and how much risk the board will accept. Second, I'd identify the technology risks the plan creates, across access, vendors, change management, cloud, and data. Third, I'd assess and prioritize them using incident data, control testing results, and a likelihood-times-impact view, ideally in dollars. Fourth, I'd recommend fixes and a 90-day roadmap with owners. I'd like to start with the incident and control data, since that shows where risk is already showing up."

Why it works

  • It answers the question that was asked: is it safe, and what comes first.
  • The buckets don't overlap and they flow: context, identify, prioritize, act.
  • It ends with a hypothesis about where to start, which hands the interviewer a natural next step.

If you blank, "people, process, technology" works as a lens inside bucket 2. On its own it doesn't say what to fix first, so tie it back to the question.

05

Exhibit 1: incident trends

Interviewer

Good place to start. The bank's security team shared incident counts for the last four quarters. Take a look. What stands out to you?

Which finding should you lead with?

Volume is not the same as risk. Phishing is flat at about 120 a quarter, which suggests the existing email controls are holding. Unauthorized access tripled, from 6 to 18. Vendor-related incidents grew more than five times, from 2 to 11. Outages after system changes rose from 3 to 8. The growth plan adds a vendor and a lot of change, so these are the trends that get worse.

Then

Say the insight out loud, tie it to the growth plan, and ask for the next piece of data.

Say it like this

"Two things stand out. Phishing is the biggest by volume, but it's flat, so the email controls seem to be working. What worries me is that unauthorized access tripled and vendor-related incidents grew more than fivefold. The growth plan adds a fintech partner and a lot of system change, so these are exactly the trends that would get worse. I'd want to know why access is failing. Do we have any control testing results?"

Notice the pattern: insight, then so-what for the client, then a hypothesis-driven ask. That ask leads the interviewer to Exhibit 2.

06

Exhibit 2: control testing

Interviewer

Good instinct. Our EY team tested the bank's IT general controls last month. Here are the results. What's your read?

What's the so-what?

Connect the exhibits. Departed employees keeping access helps explain the rise in unauthorized access. Changes going live without approval help explain the outages after changes. These are root causes, not one-off events. A cloud migration creates lots of new access and lots of change, so these controls need to work before cutover.

Then

Synthesize both exhibits in three sentences.

Say it like this

"Access and change management controls are failing about one time in four, far above the 5 percent tolerance. That lines up with Exhibit 1: people who left still have access, and changes reach production without approval, which matches the rise in unauthorized access and outages. Since the cloud migration means a lot of new access and a lot of change, I'd treat these as must-fix items before cutover."

Risk sizing: put a dollar figure on it

This is the technology risk version of market sizing. Same skills: state the formula, make clear assumptions, do clean math, then sanity-check.

Interviewer

The CRO wants a number. Estimate the exposure if customer data on the new cloud platform were breached, and tell me whether a 3 million dollar a year control program is worth it.

Customers
900,000
Records moving to cloud in phase one
60%
Cost per breached record*

10%
Chance per year with the program
4%
Program cost per year

*Case assumption covering notification, credit monitoring, legal costs, fines, and lost customers.

Your move

Say the formula before you calculate: expected annual loss equals impact of one breach times the chance it happens in a year.

  1. 1 · Records at risk900,000 customers × 60%540,000
  2. 2 · Impact of one breach (single loss)540,000 × $150$81M
  3. 3 · Expected loss per year, today$81M × 10%$8.1M
  4. 4 · Expected loss per year, with the program$81M × 4%$3.24M
  5. 5 · Value of the program$4.86M less expected loss − $3M cost+$1.86M / yr
  6. 6 · Break-even check$3M ÷ $81M ≈ 3.7 points. Pays off if breach odds fall from 10% to ≈6.3% or lower≈3.7 pts

Quick check: what's the expected annual loss today?

81 million dollars of impact times a 10 percent chance per year is 8.1 million dollars a year. 81 million is the impact of one breach, not the expected annual loss.

SLE single loss expectancy = $81M ARO annual rate of occurrence = 10% ALE = SLE × ARO = $8.1M
Then

Summarize the math in two sentences, and add one sanity check.

Say it like this

"A breach of the cloud platform would cost about 81 million dollars, which is about 8 million a year in expected losses at today's odds. The 3 million dollar program cuts that by almost 5 million, so it pays for itself. Even if I'm too optimistic about its effect, it breaks even as long as it cuts breach odds by about 4 points, and that's before counting fewer outages and a better standing with regulators."

09

The recommendation

Interviewer

The CRO just walked in. You have 60 seconds. What's your recommendation?

Your move

Lead with the answer. Then three reasons, the main risk, and next steps.

Strong answer

Answer3 reasonsRiskNext steps

"Harbor can go ahead with the digital growth plan, but it should make the cloud cutover conditional on fixing three control gaps in the next 90 days."

"First, fintech partner access. Give the partner least-privilege, time-limited access with multi-factor login and monitoring. Vendor incidents grew more than five times over the last four quarters."

"Second, user access. Remove access within 24 hours of someone leaving, and review admin accounts every quarter. Today, about one in four departed employees still have access."

"Third, change management. Nothing reaches production without documented approval. About one in four changes skip it, and outages after changes have nearly tripled."

"The program costs about 3 million dollars a year and cuts expected breach losses by almost 5 million, so it pays for itself. The main risk is that the cutover slips a few weeks, which is a fair trade. Next steps: name an owner for each fix, track key risk indicators monthly, and have us re-test the controls before go-live."

The 90-day roadmap

Days 1–30Contain
  • Remove access for everyone who has left.
  • Lock fintech partner access to least privilege, with multi-factor login.
  • Freeze production changes that lack approval.
Days 31–60Fix
  • Automate removal of access when HR records a departure.
  • Start quarterly admin access reviews.
  • Build required approvals into the release pipeline.
  • Add security terms to the partner contract.
Days 61–90Prove
  • Re-test the failed ITGCs.
  • Set a secure cloud configuration baseline.
  • Report risk indicators to the board.
  • Go or no-go decision on cutover.

Key risk indicators to track

  • Percent of departed users removed within 24 hours. Target: 100 percent.
  • Production changes without approval. Target: zero.
  • Vendor accounts using multi-factor login. Target: 100 percent.
  • High-risk findings past their due date. Target: zero.

Market sizing: SOC 2 audits

Interviewer

Estimate the annual U.S. market for SOC 2 audits.

Your move

Go top-down. Start with all businesses, narrow to the ones that need a SOC 2, then multiply by price. Say each assumption out loud.

Market growth: technology risk services

Interviewer

Why is demand for technology risk services growing, and will it keep growing?

Your move

Split growth into more buyers and more spend per buyer, then list drivers and headwinds.

Strong answer

Market growth = more buyers × more spend per buyer
Regulation

SEC rules from 2023 require public companies to disclose material cyber incidents. The EU's DORA has applied to financial firms since January 2025. State privacy laws keep spreading.

Threats

Ransomware and AI-assisted phishing raise the cost of getting it wrong.

Technology change

Cloud, AI adoption, and more vendors mean more systems and access to control.

Board accountability

Boards and regulators want evidence that controls work, not just policies.

Headwinds

  • Budget pressure in a downturn.
  • Automation and AI tools lower the cost of each test, which pushes prices down.
  • Large companies building in-house risk teams.

Quick math

If a market doubles in 5 years, what's the annual growth rate? Use the rule of 72: 72 divided by 5 is about 14 percent. The exact answer is about 14.9 percent.

"I expect continued growth, because regulation and threats keep expanding the number of buyers and what each one needs. The main risk is pricing pressure from automation, so the winners will sell judgment and remediation, not just testing."
